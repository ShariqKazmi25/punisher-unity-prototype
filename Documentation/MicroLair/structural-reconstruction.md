# Micro’s lair — structural reconstruction, revision A

This is a photograph-derived working reconstruction, not a surveyed or production floor plan. The supplied eleven screenshots are the evidence. All metric dimensions, coordinates, hidden connections, and complete outlines below are modeling assumptions. No armory is established by these references. Lighting, shaders, wear, props, and set dressing are outside this pass.

## What changes from Gemini’s plan

1. **The living area is raised.** In 08-47-16, 08-46-16, and 08-45-36 the upper stair landing continues into the aisle and the bed/chair bay. The retaining face under the living bay continues the raised platform edge. “Lower living section” is not supported as a separate floor elevation.
2. **The stairs are in gaps between enclosures.** They do not appear to converge sideways onto the center of a circular computer room. Each visible flight rises away from the lower perimeter corridor into an aisle beside the hub. The two rails in 08-45-36 belong to one flight, not two separate stairways.
3. **A rounded rectangular hub is a better starting hypothesis than a perfect cylinder.** Broad near-straight window runs, rounded ends/corners, rectangular ceiling framing, and the workstation layout favor this interpretation. A true ellipse or a different radius remains possible; perspective prevents recovery of an exact footprint.
4. **There are at least two distinct neighboring functions.** One aisle has tech on its left and living on its right (08-45-36); another has old control boards on its left and tech on its right (08-46-25 left). Placing these on opposite sides of the same hub explains both views, but is an inference rather than a proven continuous plan.
5. **Doors are aisle destinations.** The glazed door is visible beyond the living-side aisle; the red double doors terminate a tiled service aisle. The photos do not justify putting the double doors in the center of the whole back wall.
6. **Two ceiling systems coexist.** Deep concrete glass-block wells sit above a separate, damaged metal ceiling grid over the tech area. A single flat ceiling loses an important structural relationship.
7. **Neither a six-column grid nor an armory is confirmed.** Repeated photographs can show the same column more than once. Keep unseen structure provisional.

## Modeling sequence in Unity

### 1. Make a separate blockout scene

Keep the existing project and scenes intact. Create a new scene and a parent named `MicroLair_Blockout_A`, at position zero, rotation zero, scale one. Use primitives or your existing mesh tools; this plan does not require installing a package. Keep all dimensions editable. Use plain contrasting materials only to distinguish floors, frames, and structural mass.

Suggested children: `00_ReferenceCameras`, `01_LowerFloor`, `02_RaisedDecks`, `03_Stairs`, `04_ColumnsBeams`, `05_EnclosureFrames`, `06_CeilingWells`, `07_OuterShellDoors`, `08_UtilityRoutes`, `09_ScaleProxies`, `99_Provisional`.

### 2. Establish the two floor levels first

Create a lower slab centered at (0,-0.125,0) with size (18,0.25,16). Build the hub and side-bay mass up to Y=1.02. Build each upper aisle as a continuous walkable surface at the same elevation. Use separate pieces, so a later footprint correction does not require rebuilding the scene. Do not lower the bed bay beneath its adjacent aisle.

Temporarily use a square-front hub. Place 1.75 m tall human proxies on both floors and a 0.75 m tall desk in the hub. Check the rise visually before refining curves. A useful uncertainty range for the raised floor is 0.85–1.10 m.

### 3. Build the reference stair and landing

Build the right stair first. For step i=1..6, use a cube of size (2.4, 0.17*i, 0.30), centered at X=5.2, Y=(0.17*i)/2, Z=-4.8+0.30*(i-0.5). The six tops are Y=0.17, 0.34, 0.51, 0.68, 0.85, 1.02. The final tread ends exactly at Z=-3 and meets the upper aisle. There is no extra rise from the final tread to the landing.

The six-step choice is a modeling approximation; the images do not yield a reliable exact count. Preserve total rise when changing count. Keep at least 1.2 m of unobstructed upper landing beyond the top tread. Duplicate the flight to X=-5.2 only after the first view reads correctly.

For player traversal, a simple invisible ramp collider over the treads can be useful. Test it with the actual controller; do not assume that its default step offset can climb 0.17 m treads without issues. Keep collision proxies separate from visible stairs.

### 4. Replace the hub block with a rounded footprint

Use an 8 × 8 m outline with 1.6 m front corner radii. Keep the straight center of the front edge and the long side walls: do not use a cylinder as the final shape. For each quarter corner, begin with 8–12 planar segments. Curve the outer retaining face and the frame on the same outline so the enclosure rests on the slab edge.

Start the rear corners square or slightly rounded as provisional geometry. There is insufficient evidence to fix the entire back outline. Keep hub shape and radius independent of the shell. Refine the two front corners from 08-47-16 and 08-45-53, then compare the long run with 08-46-57.

### 5. Add the neighboring bays and real access gaps

Make the living bay a long shallow zone on the right of the upper right aisle. Place a 0.95 × 2.05 m bed proxy along Z, a 0.85 × 0.90 m chair near the front, and shallow locker proxies against the outer wall. These are scale checks, not final props. Keep the bay’s partition and deck visually aligned with the upper landing.

Put old control-board massing in the left bay. Do not invent a dedicated armory. Leave a 1.0–1.2 m wide opening in the hub’s side frame, starting just behind the upper stair landing, so the player can enter the workstation. A second access gap may be placed on the other aisle as a provisional design choice. Preserve access from the living aisle into the bed bay as well.

### 6. Place the columns and beams using occlusion

Use 0.8 m diameter cylinders as initial columns. Position the right foreground column so it masks part of the living-side stair and partition when viewed like 08-45-36. This is more useful than imposing a symmetrical six-column grid. Keep each column’s collision out of the intended stair width.

Add transverse and longitudinal beam masses above the columns. Verify head clearance on the upper level; with a 4.0 m beam underside, a 1.02 m platform has 2.98 m of clearance. Keep supports and beams as separate editable objects. The exact construction system cannot be solved from these photos alone.

### 7. Construct the steel-framed enclosure

Build major vertical posts first: approximately 0.9–1.2 m apart, 45–65 mm across. Add thinner horizontal glazing bars, roughly 25–40 mm across, at about 0.45–0.55 m spacing. These dimensions are starting estimates; visual grid density matters more than literal member sizes at this stage.

Carry bars around the rounded front corners with segmented arcs. Keep posts vertical and horizontal courses continuous. The reference is a broken glazed partition, not a wire mesh cage. Omit glass initially; the frame silhouette should already identify the space. Later, assign panes individually rather than wrapping the room in a continuous transparent cylinder.

### 8. Add the stair pipe rails

Use upper rails approximately 0.95 m above the local tread/landing and a lower rail about 0.45 m above. Start with 45–60 mm pipe diameter and 65–80 mm posts. Make short straight cylinders between control points, with elbow meshes or smooth curves at turns. Rounded end returns and small spherical post caps are distinctive in the photos.

The rail follows the stair slope and then curves into the upper landing. It is not part of the glazing frame. Keep posts just outside the nominal clear walking width; check the foreground column for interference.

### 9. Build the two ceiling layers

First add the thin ceiling grid near the enclosure top, Y≈3.82. Use a rectangular lattice and a sparse set of placeholder infill panels. Then add the higher concrete structure with deep openings. Model each well as four walls/reveals around an actual opening, with rounded inner corners where visible, and a glass-block grid at its top.

Start the pair of openings over the hub and keep them movable. Do not put an unbroken ceiling slab underneath the wells: missing grid panels should expose their depth. The dividing concrete member and thick curved reveals matter much more structurally than the individual glass blocks. Ceiling visibility can be toggled while working on the plan.

### 10. Close the shell only after camera checks

Place the glazed screen provisionally at the end of the right aisle. Build the red-door service aisle as a separate study until its connection is established. Add curved or chamfered tiled returns around the glazed screen. Build the outer walls in replaceable segments. Preserve a 1.8–2.4 m service passage where the tiled-wall photograph requires it; widen the shell rather than compressing the hub beyond recognition.

The rear connector and the utility corridor are unresolved. Put these objects under `99_Provisional`, and do not use them to establish a false claim about the original set. If the red-door shot cannot be matched from the proposed left aisle, move that corridor to a side/rear continuation; keep the observed adjacency (cage on one side, tiled wall on the other).

### 11. Add only large utility geometry

Add paired overhead pipe runs, elbows, a few hangers, broad control cabinets, and shallow drain channels across the lower corridor. Keep pipes below structural members where the photographs show them, while preserving upper-aisle head clearance. Start pipe diameters around 0.08–0.18 m and drain widths around 0.15–0.25 m as modeling estimates. Defer cables, tiny fittings, glass fragments and hardware.

### 12. Validate against several views before detailing

Create named cameras; all positions below are starting points, not recovered camera calibration:

- `C01_RightAisle_084536`: position (5.2,1.6,-7); look at (5.2,2.0,4). Match tech left, bed/chair right, one flight, distant glazed screen.
- `C02_HubFront_084716`: position (1.5,1.6,-7); look at (3.5,2.0,0). Match hub corner left and living-side stair right.
- `C03_LeftAisle_084625`: position (-5.2,1.6,-7); look at (-5.2,2.0,4). Match control boards left and tech right.
- `C04_ServiceDoors_084633`: use the separate service-aisle study. Looking toward the doors, place enclosure on camera-left and tiled wall on camera-right. A camera on the upper left aisle looking +Z would reverse that handedness, so do not assign these doors to that endpoint merely for symmetry. Test a reversed orientation or a right/rear continuation when further references become available.
- `C05_InsideCeiling_084604`: camera on the hub floor, looking upward through the grid to the wells.

Begin with an ordinary perspective lens around 60° vertical field of view, then adjust it before moving large structures. Match the vanishing lines, stair top, front corner tangent, column overlap, and frame grid. Cropped source photos prevent exact lens recovery. Avoid using an extreme lens to hide incorrect proportions.

Acceptance checks: walk from lower corridor up both flights; enter the hub and living bay without passing through frame bars; reach the glazed screen; verify continuous upper floor level; verify ceiling clearance; compare the three major frontal compositions; confirm no placeholder furniture forces implausibly narrow passages. Resolve the service-door handedness before declaring a production-accurate plan.

## Minimum structural kit

Straight wall segments; rounded wall returns; slab segments; rounded platform corner; stair tread block; column; longitudinal and transverse beams; straight glazing bay; curved glazing bay; open doorway bay; pipe rail segment, elbow and capped post; ceiling grid bay; deep well reveal and glass-block lid; glazed screen and double-door opening; drain strip; large pipe and hanger.

Prioritize platform height → aisle adjacency → front corner silhouette → column occlusion → enclosure grid → ceiling section. Finish these relationships before adding detailed props. A convincing reconstruction can be built from this evidence, but an exact floor plan requires more continuous footage or original drawings.
